Choose a drawing¶

molviz makes seven drawings. Three are views of one record: the circle, the line and the sequence view. The other four are a figure, a close-up, a gel and a plate. Each one answers its own question, so start from the question your reader asks.

Your reader asks Draw The call
What does the plasmid hold, and where? the circle molviz.draw(record)
What lies along this stretch? the line molviz.draw(record, span=...)
Which bases, exactly? the sequence view molviz.draw(record, span=..., sequence_view=True)
What does this step of the protocol make? a figure molviz.draw_figure(records)
Does the clone match at this base? a close-up molviz.draw(checked, span=..., sequence_view=True)
Which bands should the gel show? a gel molviz.draw_gel(gel)
What does each well hold? a plate molviz.draw_plate(plate)

Each call takes its input as a Python dict, like the record you type in the tutorial, and draw_figure takes a list of records. Each call returns a drawing, which write saves as a page or a file. In your own web page, the molviz object that the viewer script gives the page makes the same drawings with draw, drawFigure, drawGel and drawPlate, which the JavaScript API lists. Pages, files and still drawings says which drawings a reader can zoom and switch, and which output suits which use.

The examples below draw pUC19-GFP, the pUC19 vector with GFP put in by Golden Gate assembly. The plate comes from AP-1, a project that builds a library of clones on 96-well and 384-well plates. The viewer draws each example in your browser when the page opens.

The circle: what a plasmid holds¶

The circle draws a circular record whole, as a ring, so a reader sees the whole plasmid at once. Each feature is an arrow along its strand. Primers sit where they bind, and the enzymes that cut the plasmid once mark their cut sites. A circular record drawn whole opens on the circle, so the call needs no options.

drawing = molviz.draw(record)

Choose what a map shows leaves out kinds, feature types and enzymes, and lights the names that matter.

The line: what lies along a stretch¶

The line draws a record, or a span of it, along a straight line, numbered as the record numbers it. It suits a linear molecule, such as a PCR product, and a part of a plasmid, such as an insert with the primers around it. Here is the GFP insert and what flanks it, bases 341 to 1210.

drawing = molviz.draw(record, span={"start": 340, "end": 1210})

A span counts bases from 0 and leaves out the base at end, so this span holds bases 341 to 1210 as the line numbers them. Any span shorter than the whole record draws as a line. To draw a whole circular record as a line, pass map="line". Draw a stretch of the record gives the steps, a span across the origin included.

The sequence view: which bases¶

The sequence view draws the bases of both strands in rows. Features are bars under the bases, with the amino acids of each CDS over them. Primers sit above and below the strands, and cut sites cross them. Use it to show where a primer anneals, where an enzyme cuts or how a junction reads. Draw it over a short span, because a whole plasmid makes many rows. Here is the start of GFP, where the ATGA junction joins it to the vector.

drawing = molviz.draw(record, span={"start": 380, "end": 440}, sequence_view=True)

On a page, the sequence view sits beside the map or under it. In a PDF, its rows follow the map on pages of their own. Show the bases sets the span, the row length and the strands.

A figure: what a step makes¶

A figure is a still drawing of one record, or of several stacked top to bottom. It goes beside a step of a protocol. A figure draws every record with the same options, so stacked records line up, such as a vector before and after a part goes in. This figure shows the Golden Gate product. It asks for BbsI, the enzyme of the Golden Gate step. BbsI no longer cuts the product, so the figure marks no BbsI site. It also lights both junctions.

drawing = molviz.draw_figure(
    [record], enzymes=["BbsI"], highlight=["ATGA junction", "TGGC junction"]
)

Stack records into a figure stacks the two PCR products over the plasmid they make.

A close-up: whether the clone is right¶

A close-up is a still drawing of a few dozen bases around one disagreement, a place where a read differs from the record it should match. It draws the line and the sequence view over that span, with the reads on it. Here, one consensus read covers all of pUC19-GFP. At base 696, inside GFP, the record holds A and the read holds G.

A close-up has no call of its own. It is the checked record drawn over a short span, with the sequence view in one row.

drawing = molviz.draw(
    checked,
    span={"start": 665, "end": 726},
    sequence_view=True,
    bases_per_row=61,
    kinds_off=["cuts"],
    highlight=["696 A>G", "consensus.fasta"],
)
drawing.write("696.svg")

Here checked is the record with its reads and disagreements. A close-up goes into an SVG, PNG or PDF file, or into a page as the SVG text from drawing.still_svg(). In your own page, the stillSvg call of the molviz object returns the same SVG. Check a clone against its reads adds the reads and draws a close-up of each disagreement.

A gel: which bands to expect¶

A gel is a simulated agarose gel, with a ladder, sample lanes and each band at its size. It holds no record, because your code works out the sizes. Here is the colony PCR that checks pUC19-GFP, with a correct clone beside the empty vector.

drawing = molviz.draw_gel(gel)

draw_gel takes no options. Draw a gel writes this gel down and draws it.

A plate: what each well holds¶

A plate is a grid of wells, each with what it holds, given as text. Up to ten different contents each get a colour and a line of the legend. Past ten, every filled well takes one colour, and the legend counts the contents. Rest the pointer on a well to see what it holds. Here is AP-1's first plate of picked colonies, which has 384 wells. Each colour marks one quarter of the plate, and each quarter goes into a 96-well plate of its own.

drawing = molviz.draw_plate(plate)

draw_plate takes no options. molviz draws a stamp, which moves a pattern of wells from one plate into another, as two plates: the one it takes from and the one it fills. Draw a plate and a stamp builds this plate and a stamp from it.
