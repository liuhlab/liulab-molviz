How a drawing stays readable¶

A map can hold more than fits on it: a dozen features, as many primers, and a cut site for every enzyme that cuts. molviz never lets two labels overlap. A map never leaves a label out without saying so, and the sequence view leaves none out. A gel is the one exception, because it may leave out a ladder size's label without counting it. This page gives the rules a drawing keeps, so that you know what to trust in it and how to tell what it left out.

No label overlaps another¶

No label covers another label or the drawing, in the circle, the line or the sequence view. Where two labels would overlap, molviz hides one. It does not shrink the text, and it does not let labels pile up. Shrunk text is hard to read, and in a pile a label can sit closest to the wrong item. Either way, the reader can't tell that anything is wrong. A hidden label is counted instead, so the reader knows.

Every hidden label is counted¶

A drawing that hides labels says so at the bottom right of the map, counted by kind, such as "3 cut sites and 1 primer are hidden". On a page, rest the pointer on that notice to list the hidden labels. In Python, the drawing that molviz.draw returns holds the same sentence in notice and the hidden labels in hidden, so your code can report what a file left out. No notice means every label shows.

Labels hide in a fixed order, the least useful first:

  1. The label of a dimmed item hides before the label of a lit one.
  2. Cut sites hide first, then primers, reads, features and insertions. Disagreements hide last.
  3. Among cut sites, the enzyme that cuts most often hides first.
  4. Where all else is equal, the longest label hides first.

The order follows what a reader looks for. A record can carry dozens of cut sites, and an enzyme that cuts many times says the least about any one place. A unique cutter, the one a cloning plan looks for, hides last among cut sites. A disagreement between a read and the record is the point of checking a clone, so it hides last of all.

A name written on its feature's own arrow never hides, since it takes no room beside the map. The sequence view hides no label at all.

On a page, a hidden label can come back. Zoom in, and the map is laid out larger. Switch off a kind you don't need, and its room goes to the rest. The notice counts again each time. A file is laid out once, at its own size, so what it hides stays hidden, and hidden lists it.

A gel leaves out a crowded size¶

A gel writes each ladder size beside the gel, at the height where its band runs. Two sizes can run closer than a line of text. Then the gel leaves out the smaller size's label, and it does not count it, so the drawing's hidden stays empty. The gel still draws the band, and on a page, resting the pointer on the band shows its size. A sample lane's bands and the legend's lines never hide.

A highlight moves nothing¶

The highlight option names what a reader should look at. Everything else dims to one pale grey, and nothing moves. Each item stays where it would be with no highlight, so a lit drawing and a plain one line up item for item. A lit label is the last to hide.

Below, pUC19-GFP is drawn twice as a line: first plain, then with GFP and its two primers lit. Every arrow and label stays in place.

Colours follow the feature type¶

A feature takes its colour from its type. Coding sequences are blue, promoters and other regulatory parts orange, terminators pink and origins yellow. Other groups of types have colours of their own, and a type in no group is light grey. The record format lists the types in each group. The colours come from Paul Tol's light scheme. Tol made it so that readers with colour blindness can tell its colours apart, and so that black text reads on each of them.

Primers are purple and insertions are red. A colour you give a feature, or one of its segments, is used instead of the colour of its type. A Sanger trace draws its four curves in the colours that trace viewers use: A green, C blue, G black and T red.

A drawing keeps a white ground¶

A drawing is always drawn on white, even on a page with a dark theme. Every colour above was chosen against white, so the drawing reads the same on any page and prints the same.

Positions read as the record numbers them¶

Every label and every hover counts from 1 and includes both ends, as SnapGene and GenBank do. A line drawn from part of a record keeps the record's numbers, and a stretch across the origin of a circular record runs from the last base on to 1. Coordinates explains how a record writes these positions.

Text you can find, files that look the same anywhere¶

On a page, every base and every label is real text, so the browser's find, select and copy work on it. In an SVG, PNG or PDF file, each letter is drawn as an outline instead. The file then looks the same in every program and needs no font. Pages, files and still drawings says which to choose.
