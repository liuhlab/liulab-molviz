Coordinates¶

A record counts its bases from 0, and a span {start, end} holds the bases from start up to end, but not the base at end. A reader never sees these numbers. Every label and every hover counts from 1 and includes both ends, as SnapGene and GenBank do. molviz writes them that way. This page explains why the record counts one way and the drawing another, and how a span crosses the origin of a circular record. The record format states each rule in full.

A span counts from 0 and stops before its end¶

In pUC19-GFP, GFP is the span {"start": 395, "end": 1112}. Counted from 0, it holds the bases at 395 to 1111. A reader sees it as 396 .. 1112.

Counting this way keeps the arithmetic plain:

  • A span's length is end - start, here 717 bases, with no 1 to add.
  • Two spans that touch share a number. The lac promoter in pUC19-GFP is three segments, from 1173 to 1180, 1180 to 1198 and 1198 to 1204. Each starts where the one before it ends.
  • It is how Python slices text, so bases[395:1112] gives GFP's bases. Biopython, BED files and the lab's cloning tools count the same way, so your code can hand its numbers over as they are.

Counting from 1 with both ends included is how people read a map. A person says "base 396", not "the base at index 395". So the record keeps the form that code gets right, and molviz turns it into the form people read when it writes a label.

A point between bases is the count of bases before it¶

A cut, an insertion, and a disagreement where a read adds bases all lie between two bases, not on one. Such a point is the number of bases before it. That number is also the position, from 1, of the base it follows. So a cut after base 406 is 406, and its label reads (406) with no change. On a circular record the origin is 0, never the record's length.

A single base, such as a base a primer does not pair with, is its index from 0. It is always below the record's length, even inside a span across the origin.

A span across the origin ends past the length¶

A circular record has no real start. Its origin is only where the numbering begins, and a site or a feature can lie over it. Such a span lets end pass the record's length. pUC19-GFP is 3,347 bp, and its BsmBI site is {"start": 3343, "end": 3349}: the last four bases and the first two. A reader sees it as 3344 .. 2.

There were two other ways to write it, and both cost more:

  • An end lower than its start, as SnapGene prints it. Then every length, and every test of whether a span holds a base, needs a second case for a span across the origin.
  • Two spans, split at the origin. Then the site is no longer one span. Nothing in the data would tell it apart from a feature that really is made of two pieces.

With end past the length, end - start is still the span's length, here 6. A span runs at most once around the record.

The span option follows the same rule. With {"start": 3300, "end": 3400}, the drawing below is the 100 bases across pUC19-GFP's origin, drawn as a line. The ruler goes from 3340 on to 10, the thick tick marks the origin, and the title gives the stretch as 3301 .. 53. One BsmBI cut lies just past the origin, at 3.

A joined feature's segments run in order¶

A feature can be made of several segments. In pUC19-GFP, lacZα has two, from 145 to 395 and from 1112 to 1130, because GFP was put in between them. A feature lists its segments in order of their starts. On a circular record, a segment after the origin is counted past the length, as a span across the origin is.

On a linear record, a feature may run off the end and on again from the start, but only once. That happens on a PCR product that starts inside a gene, as when a plasmid's backbone is amplified from a primer inside lacZα. The one segment that starts lower than the segment before it marks where the feature runs off the end. Splitting such a feature in two would show the reader two features where SnapGene shows one, and break its translation.
